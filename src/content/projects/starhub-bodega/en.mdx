## Context

StarHub is the tool a warehouse operator uses to record a container's arrival from their phone. They scan the receipt code, log every line as it is unloaded, note where each pallet goes, and photograph damaged goods.

This project is the backend behind that Android app. The Android client was led by someone else on the team, with contributions from me.

The project came from a warehouse need: that the ERP's inventory reflect what is actually in the warehouse at the moment goods are unloaded, not hours later.

## Problem

Goods-in was recorded on paper and someone transcribed it into the ERP later. Until that transcription happened, the system's inventory did not match what was in the warehouse.

That gap has a commercial cost: quotes go out against stock that has already changed, and a discrepancy only surfaces when someone goes to find the goods and they are not where the system says.

Transcription also loses detail. Pallet positions and the condition of damaged goods rarely survive the trip from paper to system.

## Technical decisions

The ERP generates receipt numbers with formulas of its own that take no lock. Two operators working at once would have been handed the same sequence number. It also has notifications and automatic actions on those tables, which a direct write would not have fired.

Reads go over direct SQL against the ERP's database, which is the fast path. Writes go through its API, which is slower but respects those formulas and those triggers. The latency was accepted because the alternative was an inventory with repeated sequence numbers.

No route in the service writes over SQL. Every write leaves through a single API client, which is the only point in the code that issues one.

## Architecture

A phone cannot authenticate with the platform's identity, because enrolment happens before the device holds any credential at all. That is the service's gate: a single public endpoint, with four rules applied at once.

Once enrolled, the device works with a token scoped to its warehouse, and from there it reads over SQL and writes through the ERP's API.

## Result

Goods-in reaches the ERP at the moment the operator records it, with its lines, each pallet's position and the photos of damaged goods. Inventory stops depending on a later transcription.

A smoke test starts the server and makes real requests against it. It covers TLS, credentials, warehouse filtering, querying and response mapping. It uses no test doubles and writes no data, so it checks the integration rather than an imitation of it.

Alongside it there are 30 `node:test` cases covering pagination and the device lifecycle.

## What I learned

The enrolment registry stayed inside the process, so the service cannot take a second instance until it moves to shared storage. That move is written down as the next change.

It is a decision that works with one warehouse and breaks with the second. Keeping state in the process costs nothing to write and costs a redesign the day the business grows.

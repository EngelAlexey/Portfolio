## Context

StarHub is the tool a warehouse operator uses to record a container's arrival from their phone. They scan the receipt code, log every line as it is unloaded, note where each pallet goes, and photograph damaged goods.

The service and the Android app were built by the team. My assignment was maintenance: fixing the defects that surfaced once the system was already in production. On the app, which another colleague had started, I also connected the screens to the service's real data and added the timestamp seal on evidence photos.

## Problem

Goods-in was recorded on paper and someone transcribed it into the ERP later. Until that happened, inventory did not match what was in the warehouse, and the discrepancy only surfaced when someone went to find the goods and they were not where the system said.

Transcription also loses detail. Pallet positions and the condition of damaged goods rarely survive the trip from paper to system.

## Technical decisions

The database schema is not controlled by this service. It is governed by the tool the client runs its operation from, and it can change without the service hearing about it. All the maintenance work starts from that.

Database names are translated with SQL aliases inside the data-access layer, rather than propagated to the contract the Android client consumes. That contract stays stable and absorbs a schema change without shipping a new release: the build already tested in production keeps serving untouched. The correspondence between columns is checked against the real data before it is written, because two columns of the same type are easy to cross.

The warehouse queue filters by document type. The table it reads mixes receipts and dispatches, and without that filter an operator would see outbound orders looking like receipts and try to receive cargo against one of them.

A field the database stops offering comes back null, not padded. If that takes away functionality the operation was using, it gets reported as such rather than hidden inside the mapping.

## Architecture

The service reads over direct SQL against the ERP's database, which is the fast path, and writes only through the ERP's API, slower but respecting the sequence formulas and the triggers a direct write would not fire. That separation already existed, and the maintenance work had to respect it.

A phone cannot authenticate with the platform's identity, because enrolment happens before the device holds any credential at all. That is the service's gate, and it carries four rules applied at once.

Once enrolled, the device works with a token scoped to its warehouse.

## Result

With StarHub, goods-in reaches the ERP at the moment the operator records it, with its lines, each pallet's position and the photos of damaged goods. Inventory stopped depending on a later transcription.

A change in the ERP's schema is absorbed inside the service, with no new release pushed to the phones already on the floor. The queue shows receipts and only receipts, even though they share a table with dispatches.

One case was written down as unverified, because checking it creates real rows in the ERP and needs the client's authorisation.

## What I learned

A service integrated against a platform another team governs does not control its schema. Putting the translation in the mapping layer, and not in the contract the mobile client consumes, is what avoids pushing a new release to phones already out in the field.

The other half is commercial. When a schema change takes away something the operation was using, reporting it is part of the job. Quietly resolving it inside the mapping leaves the people running the operation unaware they lost anything.

## Context

Seiri is the attendance clock-in app Kaizen offers to the companies that use its systems. Staff clock in and out from their phone, with a photo and a location, and that record is used to calculate payroll.

A good share of those staff work on site and on the road, where there is no time clock and no guaranteed signal. The project takes the clock-in point to where the person is, instead of making them go to a fixed one.

## Problem

With no system, arrivals are written in a notebook or taken down by a supervisor name by name. At sites with a lot of staff, that queue takes productive time every morning, before anyone reaches their post.

That record is not reliable on its own either. The time is whatever someone wrote down, there is no evidence of where the person was and payroll is calculated on data that somebody has to confirm later.

## Technical decisions

Offline, two devices can clock in the same person without seeing each other. On reconnecting, both send an entry that was valid according to what each one observed. The earlier scheme counted the day's entries and then inserted the new one: two steps with no guarantee between them.

Validation moved into a database transaction that locks the day's rows before deciding. The second sync therefore waits for the first to finish.

The entry that loses the conflict is flagged for review. Discarding it silently would leave someone with no shift on record and no way to dispute it.

Recording a shift cannot depend on a supporting service being available. So every entry stores the outcome of the identity check, and later review works from that record.

## Architecture

The phone writes to its local database first and puts the entry in a queue. A background task sends the queue to the API when there is a connection. The token is kept in the system's secure store.

The four rules that decide whether an entry is accepted are applied on the server, inside the same transaction.

## Result

Staff clock in within seconds, with a photo and a location, with nobody taking notes and without depending on signal. The entry is sent on its own as soon as there is a connection.

Every record arrives with its time, its location and its identity check, so payroll is calculated on data that does not need confirming afterwards.

The geofence formula exists twice, on the server and on the client. One test has the sole job of failing if the two versions stop agreeing.

## What I learned

Counting the day's entries on the client does not prevent a duplicate, because each device keeps its own count and neither sees the other while offline.

Inside the server transaction the conflict still happens, but it is detected and sent to review instead of being settled by whoever arrives first. With clients that work offline, the check that decides has to sit on the server, the one point that sees every client.
